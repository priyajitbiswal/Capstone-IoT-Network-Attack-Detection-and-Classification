\begin{abstract}
Machine learning (ML) models evaluated on modern Internet of Things (IoT) network intrusion detection benchmarks, particularly CICIoT2023, frequently report classification accuracies ranging from 98.5\% to 99.9\% under standard random splitting protocols. In this paper, we conduct a forensic data integrity audit and empirical evaluation demonstrating that reported headline metrics in the surveyed literature reflect unpartitioned identical-feature data leakage, conflicting label collisions, severe class imbalance, and an absence of cross-session temporal validation. Across a stratified sample of 529,562 flow records, we identify 11.29\% feature redundancy (59,780 flows) and 8,740 conflicting-label collision hashes spanning 28,608 rows (5.40\% of the sample), where mathematically identical 39-feature coordinate vectors map simultaneously to two or more conflicting ground-truth labels (predominantly between DoS and DDoS variants of identical protocols, with rare boundary collisions involving benign traffic). We show that simulated random 80/20 train/test splits---the predominant evaluation regime in the surveyed literature---allow 81.39\% of test collision instances (and 10.14\% of un-deduplicated test flows) to match identical feature coordinates in the training partition, creating an empirical opportunity for classifiers to predict labels via coordinate memorization rather than learning generalized traffic patterns. To prevent this, we introduce an atomic SHA-256 group-hash partitioning protocol that guarantees strictly zero inter-partition feature overlap alongside strict preprocessor isolation. We evaluate five representative model families across 21 configurations spanning Binary (2-class), Category (8-class), and Fine-Grained (34-class) tasks. Under zero leakage, decision tree ensembles establish leak-free diagnostic baselines: Random Forest achieves 89.61\% binary accuracy (Macro-F1 0.7690; closely followed by LightGBM at 89.15\%, Macro-F1 0.7637), while gradient-boosted trees (LightGBM) achieve 78.93\% category accuracy (Macro-F1 0.6894) and 72.65\% fine-grained accuracy (Macro-F1 0.6145). We analyze the operational trade-offs of class weighting: unweighted empirical risk minimization increases overall test accuracy (+1.80\%) by favoring volumetric majority floods, but triggers a 79\% to 82\% relative recall reduction on minority web attacks (SQL Injection: 35.83\% $\to$ 7.48\%; XSS: 20.22\% $\to$ 3.83\%) and increases false negatives to Benign by +125.31\% (1,517 $\to$ 3,418). Finally, temporal evaluation on an independent clean cross-capture holdout ($N=57,280$) recorded during subsequent physical testbed sessions reveals notable performance degradation (binary Macro-F1 drops from 0.7637 to 0.7034), indicating that classifiers capture session-specific burst dynamics. Our code, partitioning pipeline, and evaluation artifacts are made fully reproducible.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The rapid proliferation of Internet of Things (IoT) infrastructure has expanded the operational attack surface. Billions of edge devices---including smart home sensors, surveillance endpoints, industrial actuators, and healthcare gateways---are interfaced directly with public or unsegmented local networks. Due to hardware footprint constraints, legacy unauthenticated protocols, and infrequent firmware patching, IoT networks remain susceptible to malicious exploitation. Threats range from multi-gigabit Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) botnets (such as Mirai and its variants) to stealthy, low-footprint application exploits including SQL Injection (SQLi), Cross-Site Scripting (XSS), Command Injection, and unauthorized remote backdoor execution~\cite{neto2023ciciot, ahmad2021network}.

To defend these heterogeneous perimeters, network intrusion detection systems (NIDS) increasingly rely on machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) models trained on tabular flow telemetry~\cite{mishra2019detailed}. The research community has widely adopted modern large-scale benchmarks, foremost among them the \textbf{CICIoT2023} dataset published by the Canadian Institute for Cybersecurity~\cite{neto2023ciciot}. Comprising over 46 million raw network flows generated on a physical testbed of 105 IoT devices across 33 attack profiles, CICIoT2023 serves as a central reference for IoT security benchmarking.

\subsection{The Accuracy Paradox in Tabular IoT Benchmarks}
In recent literature, published empirical evaluations on CICIoT2023 frequently report high diagnostic capability. As documented in our literature survey (Section~\ref{sec:related_work}), several recent peer-reviewed studies evaluating CICIoT2023 report classification accuracies ranging from \textbf{98.5\% to 99.9\%} across diverse classifiers including Random Forests, XGBoost, LightGBM, 1D-CNNs, and hybrid Transformer networks under their respective evaluation protocols~\cite{neto2023ciciot, khan2026resource, almahaqeri2026optimized, ullah2026comparative, abdelrahman2026feature}.

However, there is an established disparity between laboratory metrics and operational reliability. When deployed in live networks, ML-based NIDS frequently experience high false-positive rates on normal traffic, low sensitivity to low-rate application exploits, and diagnostic decay over time. In this paper, we identify three structural factors that influence benchmark metrics:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Feature Redundancy and Random-Split Leakage}: In flow datasets evaluated under standard random train/test splits---the primary evaluation protocol across the surveyed literature---repeated and duplicate feature vectors are partitioned across both training and test sets. Classifiers evaluate on samples with coordinates identical to those observed during training, creating an unmeasured opportunity for coordinate memorization.
    \item \textbf{Class Imbalance and Aggregate Metrics}: In raw benchmark captures, volumetric flooding dominates aggregate telemetry, while in our 529,562-flow stratified evaluation sample, DoS and DDoS floods constitute 56.02\% of all records (296,655 flows), whereas stealthy application threats represent rare subsets (e.g., Uploading Attack comprises 0.079\% [416 flows], XSS 0.234\% [1,239 flows], and SQL Injection 0.324\% [1,718 flows]). A classifier that minimizes unweighted error can maximize aggregate accuracy while failing to detect low-prevalence intrusions.
    \item \textbf{Absence of Cross-Session Temporal Validation}: Benchmark evaluations typically test models on random holdout slices drawn from the exact same network capture session. This fails to assess whether classifiers learn stable attack signatures or fit the transient burst characteristics of a specific capture session.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Research Questions and Contributions}
To investigate these phenomena, this paper formulates the following \textbf{Central Research Question}:
\begin{quote}
\textit{When identical-feature data leakage and class imbalance artifacts are rigorously controlled through atomic hash-grouped partitioning and cost-sensitive optimization, what is the baseline diagnostic capability, minority attack recall, and temporal stability of standard machine learning architectures on the CICIoT2023 benchmark?}
\end{quote}

We investigate this question through five structured Sub-Research Questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1 (Integrity)}: To what extent do identical feature vectors, exact duplicates, and conflicting label collisions exist in CICIoT2023, and what fraction of test instances overlap under random splitting?
    \item \textbf{RQ2 (Zero-Leakage Baseline)}: What are the measured diagnostic boundaries of linear, tree, and neural baselines across Binary (2-class), Category (8-class), and Fine-Grained (34-class) tasks when feature leakage is strictly eliminated?
    \item \textbf{RQ3 (Ambiguity Mechanics)}: How does the presence of conflicting label collisions affect tree and linear models, and does collision purging improve clean generalization?
    \item \textbf{RQ4 (Operational Trade-Offs)}: How does standard unweighted empirical loss minimization trade minority attack detection for overall headline accuracy?
    \item \textbf{RQ5 (Temporal Fragility)}: How robust are trained models when evaluated on an independent capture session recorded in subsequent capture files on the physical IoT testbed?
\end{itemize}

This manuscript delivers \textbf{four consolidated research contributions}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Forensic Data Integrity and Leakage Audit}: We perform a granular census of 529,562 stratified CICIoT2023 flow records. We find that 11.29\% of flows share repeated feature vectors, and 8,740 unique 39-feature coordinates represent \textit{conflicting label collisions} spanning 28,608 rows (where identical vectors carry conflicting ground-truth labels, predominantly between DoS and DDoS attack variants, with minimal benign involvement). We demonstrate empirically that simulated 80/20 random stratified splitting allows 81.39\% of test collision instances (and 10.14\% of un-deduplicated test flows) to match identical training coordinates.
    \item \textbf{Reproducible Zero-Leakage Group-Hash Partitioning Standard}: We formulate an open-source atomic SHA-256 group-hash partitioning protocol guaranteeing strictly zero identical-feature overlap ($\mathcal{X}_{\text{train}} \cap \mathcal{X}_{\text{test}} = \emptyset$) alongside strict preprocessor isolation. Across 21 models and three granularities, our partitions exhibit consistent generalization ($|\Delta_{\text{gen}}| \le 0.0079$), establishing verified leak-free baselines (89.61\% binary, 78.93\% category, 72.65\% fine-grained).
    \item \textbf{Empirical Analysis of Ambiguity Filtering Mechanics}: We evaluate classifier sensitivity to conflicting label noise. Decomposed validation on 72,390 common-clean instances shows that purging collision vectors yields a marginal +0.0022 Macro-F1 improvement (+0.22\%), while classifiers achieve ~41.73\% accuracy on isolated ambiguous vectors due to the non-injective nature of the stripped feature space.
    \item \textbf{Quantitative Trade-Offs: Minority Recall Collapse and Temporal Non-Stationarity}: We quantify the operational trade-offs of class weighting: unweighted training raises headline accuracy by +1.80\% but causes a 79\% to 82\% relative recall reduction on minority web attacks and increases missed intrusions to Benign by +125.31\%. Furthermore, temporal evaluation across distinct physical capture sessions reveals measurable performance degradation (binary Macro-F1 drops from 0.7637 to 0.7034), indicating sensitivity to session-specific network dynamics.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work and Research Gap Analysis}
\label{sec:related_work}

Network Intrusion Detection Systems for IoT environments have been studied across diverse benchmarks, including NSL-KDD, UNSW-NB15, BoT-IoT, ToN\_IoT, and CICIoT2023~\cite{ahmad2021network, mishra2019detailed, booij2022toniot}.

\subsection{Thematic Audit of Recent Literature}
To contextualize our findings, we conducted a systematic methodological audit of 14 peer-reviewed research papers published between 2021 and 2026 evaluating IoT NIDS models (detailed in Table~\ref{tab:lit_matrix}). These works fall into four primary methodological themes:

\subsubsection{Theme A: Tree Ensembles and Feature Selection}
Gradient-boosted decision trees (XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost) and Random Forests are widely evaluated for tabular flow analysis due to their non-linear decision boundaries and inference efficiency. Khan et al.~\cite{khan2026resource} combined Boruta feature selection with Greedy Forward Selection (GFS) on CICIoT2023 and ToN\_IoT, pruning feature sets to 10--15 attributes while reporting 99.72\%--99.94\% accuracy. Almahaqeri et al.~\cite{almahaqeri2026optimized} evaluated optimized GBDTs on CICIoT2023, reporting 99.61\% binary accuracy and 0.355\,$\mu$s latency. Abdelrahman et al.~\cite{abdelrahman2026feature} introduced FEC-IDS, achieving 99.76\% binary and 98.88\% 34-class accuracy using tree-based feature rankings. While these works reduce feature dimensionalities, none audited identical-feature vector collisions across splits or evaluated temporal session stability.

\subsubsection{Theme B: Deep Sequence and Recurrent Architectures}
Several studies explore deep learning to model temporal or inter-feature correlations. Gulzar and Mustafa~\cite{gulzar2025enhancing} proposed DeepCLG (combining 1D-CNN, LSTM, and GRU layers), achieving 99.82\% accuracy on BoT-IoT but experiencing performance drops down to 57.77\% on UNSW-NB15. Qaddos et al.~\cite{qaddos2024novel} deployed a 1D-CNN-BiLSTM architecture with attention mechanisms, reporting $>$99\% accuracy on BoT-IoT. While deep architectures offer expressive representation learning, their computational overhead poses challenges for edge deployment, and evaluations were restricted to in-distribution random splits.

\subsubsection{Theme C: Federated Learning and Edge Privacy}
Federated learning (FL) frameworks train collaborative NIDS without aggregating raw network telemetry. Moussaoui et al.~\cite{moussaoui2026resilient} evaluated a CNN-LSTM architecture under Dirichlet non-IID client distributions on CICIoT2023, observing that F1 dropped from 97.36\% in centralized training to 71.53\% under client skew ($\alpha=0.01$). Al-Hejri et al.~\cite{alhejri2026improving} combined CNNs with Vision Transformers in an FL pipeline, reporting a drop from 94.89\% to 69.35\% under non-IID conditions. Fenanir and Semchedine~\cite{fenanir2023smart} and Ullah et al.~\cite{ullah2026comparative} explored edge federated aggregation across IoT gateways. These studies highlight the sensitivity of classifiers to distribution shifts, though they did not evaluate cross-capture temporal drift on identical testbeds.

\subsubsection{Theme D: Benchmark Generation and Baselining}
The foundational CICIoT2023 study by Neto et al.~\cite{neto2023ciciot} benchmarked eight ML/DL algorithms across 105 devices, reporting 99.16\% accuracy with Random Forest. However, their DNN baseline exhibited low minority recall: 14.7\% on Dictionary Brute Force and 3.8\% on Web attacks. Booij et al.~\cite{booij2022toniot} and Moustafa~\cite{moustafa2021distributed} evaluated ToN\_IoT, demonstrating that models trained with static IP addresses fail to generalize across network environments. Essop et al.~\cite{essop2021dataset} and Umar et al.~\cite{umar2025energy} addressed dataset generation fidelity and knowledge distillation for edge deployment.

\subsection{The Research Gap in the Reviewed Sample}
Within the 14 surveyed studies, we observe specific methodological patterns:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Collision Auditing}: None of the 14 surveyed studies reported an audit of identical-feature vector collisions across train and test splits. While some studies removed exact duplicate full rows ($X, y$), identical feature coordinates ($X$) carrying conflicting labels ($y$) were not addressed.
    \item \textbf{Temporal Cross-Capture Evaluation}: None of the 14 surveyed studies evaluated trained models on distinct temporal capture sessions recorded on the same physical testbed. Evaluations relied on random holdouts or cross-validation.
    \item \textbf{Metric Selection}: Studies frequently emphasized aggregate accuracy, which can obscure low recall on minority attack categories in heavily imbalanced flow datasets.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Dataset Integrity, Collisions, and Leakage Analysis}
\label{sec:dataset_integrity}

\subsection{The CICIoT2023 Benchmark and Feature Space}
The CICIoT2023 dataset was generated by deploying 105 IoT devices executing 33 distinct attack profiles and benign network behaviors. Network traffic was captured via port mirroring and processed into bidirectional flow statistics. While raw packet captures contain link-layer and network-layer identifiers (MAC addresses, IP addresses, port numbers, and timestamps), the benchmark feature extraction protocol extracts 39 numerical statistical flow features over fixed packet windows, omitting link-layer and network-layer identifiers~\cite{neto2023ciciot}.

The resulting public tabular schema consists of \textbf{39 numerical features} plus a ground-truth label, encompassing flow duration, packet/byte rates, packet length statistics, protocol flag counters, and inter-arrival times (IAT).

These conflicting hashes span \textbf{28,608 rows (5.40\%)} in the full sample and \textbf{18,416 rows (3.68\%)} in the unique stratum.

Table~\ref{tab:collision_taxonomy} catalogs dominant collision groups. Because each unique 39-feature hash is assigned to its exact set of associated labels, the rows represent mutually disjoint collision subsets that sum additively to the 8,740 unique collision hashes and 28,608 total collision flows. A structural contributor to DoS and DDoS feature collisions is the composition of the benchmark feature set, which consists of statistical flow counters and rate metrics without source or destination IP addresses, MAC addresses, or timestamps~\cite{neto2023ciciot}. The omission of source-entropy and topological identifiers eliminates the information needed to distinguish single-source flooding (DoS) from distributed multi-source botnets (DDoS). Consequently, single-packet TCP datagrams or SYN handshakes produced by a single attacker can possess identical 39-feature statistical values as those produced across a distributed botnet. In total, DoS versus DDoS collisions sharing identical protocols account for 8,433 out of 8,740 conflicting hashes (96.49\%) and 26,697 out of 28,608 collision flows (93.32\%). In contrast, collisions directly involving BenignTraffic are minimal, comprising only 41 unique coordinate hashes (0.47\% of collision hashes) and 621 flows (2.17\% of collision flows, representing 0.11\% of the total dataset), primarily between standard ARP exchanges and spoofed ARP replies (\texttt{BenignTraffic} $\leftrightarrow$ \texttt{MITM-ArpSpoofing}). This indicates that while the 39-feature space of CICIoT2023 is non-injective for high-rate flooding profiles, benign-attack boundary ambiguity is concentrated in specific low-level protocol exchanges rather than widespread across the feature space.

\section{Leakage-Controlled Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Atomic SHA-256 Hash-Grouped Partitioning}
To prevent identical-feature data leakage, we formulate an \textbf{Atomic Hash-Grouped Partitioning Protocol}. 
Let $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{39}$ denote the feature vector of flow $i$. We compute a 64-character SHA-256 hash digest over the canonical string representation of $\mathbf{x}_i$:
\begin{equation}
h_i = \text{SHA-256}\left(\bigoplus_{j=1}^{39} \text{repr}(x_{i,j})\right)
\end{equation}
All flows sharing the identical hash $h_i$ form an atomic group $\mathcal{G}_k$. Each atomic group is assigned in its entirety to either the training set ($\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$), validation set ($\mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}$), or test set ($\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}$) using a stratified hash assignment algorithm that preserves class proportions while strictly guaranteeing:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{X}_{\text{train}} \cap \mathcal{X}_{\text{val}} = \emptyset, \quad \mathcal{X}_{\text{train}} \cap \mathcal{X}_{\text{test}} = \emptyset, \quad \mathcal{X}_{\text{val}} \cap \mathcal{X}_{\text{test}} = \emptyset
\end{equation}

\subsection{Partition Formulations: Strategy 1 vs. Strategy 2}
We formulate two controlled partitioning regimes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Strategy 1 (Primary / Collision-Contaminated)}: Retains all 501,098 unique stratum flows, partitioning them into Train ($N = 350,821$; 70\%), Validation ($N = 75,172$; 15\%), and Internal Test ($N = 75,105$; 15\%). This reflects operational conditions where conflicting label noise exists in flow traffic.
    \item \textbf{Strategy 2 (Ablation / Collision-Purged)}: Purges all 8,740 conflicting collision hashes, leaving 482,682 flows partitioned into Train ($N = 337,838$), Validation ($N = 72,390$), and Internal Test ($N = 72,454$).
\end{itemize}

The validation set of Strategy 2 ($N = 72,390$) represents the exact \textbf{Common-Clean Validation Population} embedded within Strategy 1. The remaining 2,782 validation flows in Strategy 1 constitute the \textbf{Ambiguous Collision Validation Population}, enabling decomposed error routing.

\subsection{Temporal Cross-Capture Clean Holdout Design and Provenance}
To evaluate same-testbed cross-session temporal stability, we constructed an independent holdout set of \textbf{$N = 57,280$ flows} from subsequent capture archives (`Merged26.csv` through `Merged40.csv`). In the CICIoT2023 distribution, network flows were captured sequentially from the same physical 105-device testbed and segmented across numbered CSV archives. While our primary training and internal evaluation sample was extracted from early archives (`Merged01.csv` through `Merged25.csv`), this holdout draws strictly from later files (`Merged26.csv` through `Merged40.csv`). Because individual flow records in the benchmark omit absolute wall-clock timestamps, this holdout evaluates \textit{same-testbed cross-session temporal stability} across capture files rather than out-of-domain cross-topology transfer. To prevent coordinate contamination, all feature hashes present in the training partition were purged from this holdout, guaranteeing \textbf{strictly zero identical-feature overlap ($0.00\%$)} across all 39 coordinates.

\subsection{Preprocessor Isolation and Model Configurations}
To prevent distributional leakage, all preprocessing transformations---including finite rate clipping limits and `StandardScaler` moments ($\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\sigma}^2$)---were fitted strictly on $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$ and serialized. 

We benchmarked five representative model families across three diagnostic granularities (21 configurations total), implemented using scikit-learn~\cite{pedregosa2011scikit} and PyTorch~\cite{paszke2019pytorch}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{SGD Logistic Regression}: Linear classifier trained via mini-batch stochastic gradient descent with log-loss and $L_2$ regularization.
    \item \textbf{PyTorch Tabular MLP}: Multi-layer perceptron (Input[39] $\to$ Dense[128] $\to$ BatchNorm $\to$ ReLU $\to$ Dropout[0.2] $\to$ Dense[64] $\to$ BatchNorm $\to$ ReLU $\to$ Output[$K$]) trained with AdamW under a standard 5-epoch CPU budget.
    \item \textbf{Random Forest (RF)}: Ensemble of 50 decorrelated decision trees with max depth 15 and balanced bootstrapping~\cite{breiman2001random}.
    \item \textbf{LightGBM}: Leaf-wise gradient boosted trees with GOSS sampling and max depth 12~\cite{ke2017lightgbm}.
    \item \textbf{XGBoost}: Depth-wise gradient boosted trees with exact split finding and histogram binning~\cite{chen2016xgboost}.
\end{enumerate}

To address class imbalance, models were optimized using cost-sensitive inverse-frequency class weighting:
\begin{equation}
W_c = \frac{N_{\text{train}}}{K \cdot N_c}
\end{equation}
where $K$ is the number of classes and $N_c$ is the support of class $c$.

\subsection{Host-Side Computational Profiling}
Table~\ref{tab:computational_profiling} and Figure~\ref{fig:latency_pareto} benchmark host-side CPU inference latency, batch throughput, and memory consumption on an Intel Core i5-1135G7 CPU (2.40 GHz, 4 cores, 8 threads, 16 GB RAM) running Python 3.13.

It is critical to distinguish between sequential single-flow latency and batch inference throughput, as they measure fundamentally different operational dimensions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Single-Flow P50 and P95 Latency}: Latency is measured by issuing individual, unbatched inference requests sequentially (\texttt{model.predict(x[i:i+1])}) across 2,000 single-flow vectors (500 warmup iterations, 5 runs) using \texttt{src\_v2/evaluate.py}. This measures the host-side turnaround time experienced when an isolated flow arrives at the host decision engine, capturing full Python interpreter and framework dispatch overhead.
    \item \textbf{Batch Inference Throughput}: Throughput (flows per second) is measured by passing pre-allocated in-memory NumPy feature arrays in large batches (batch size 10,000, 5 runs) directly to model vectorized prediction routines (\texttt{.predict()}). This amortizes Python interpreter dispatch, function call overhead, and memory allocation across the batch, evaluating upper-bound algorithmic throughput in memory.
    \item \textbf{Operational Scope and Physical Constraints}: Batch throughput does not represent single-flow latency (which cannot be obtained simply by taking the inverse of batch throughput) nor does it represent network interface line rate. Network packet capture, link-layer parsing, flow reconstruction, sliding-window statistical calculation, and feature vector assembly occur upstream of the ML inference engine and incur substantial independent computational overhead. Furthermore, these host-side CPU benchmarks reflect desktop/server architectures; deployment on resource-constrained physical microcontrollers (e.g., ARM Cortex-M or ESP32) will incur tighter memory, cache, and cycle limitations.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Re-Evaluating Published Performance on CICIoT2023}
Our empirical findings provide context for understanding reported performance on CICIoT2023:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Feature Overlap}: Under unpartitioned random splits, 81.39\% of test collision instances match training coordinates, creating an empirical potential for coordinate memorization in high-capacity classifiers.
    \item \textit{Class Imbalance Masking}: High-volume flooding traffic masks low detection rates on stealthy exploits when evaluations rely on overall accuracy.
    \item \textit{Temporal Sensitivity}: Evaluating on slices from the same capture session does not evaluate cross-session stability and burst non-stationarity across subsequent capture files.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Practical Recommendations for IoT NIDS Evaluation}
Based on these findings, we highlight four recommendations for evaluating IoT intrusion detection systems:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Implement Hash-Grouped Partitioning}: Evaluators should verify that $\mathcal{X}_{\text{train}} \cap \mathcal{X}_{\text{test}} = \emptyset$ to prevent coordinate leakage across repeated flow vectors.
    \item \textbf{Report Class-Disaggregated Metrics}: Reporting macro-averaged F1, Matthew's correlation, and minority attack recall prevents aggregate metrics from obscuring performance on stealthy threats.
    \item \textbf{Incorporate Multi-Stage Filtering Architectures}: Fast linear models can serve as initial filters for obvious volumetric traffic, routing ambiguous flows to deeper tree ensembles for fine-grained attribution.
    \item \textbf{Account for Flow Feature Limitations}: Tabular flow statistics lack packet payload inspection. Detecting application-layer exploits such as SQL Injection and XSS necessitates coupling flow summaries with targeted payload inspection.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Threats to Validity and Limitations}
\label{sec:threats}

We explicitly delineate the scientific boundaries of this investigation:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dataset Scope}: Benchmark evaluations were conducted on the CICIoT2023 dataset. Cross-dataset transferability to other IoT datasets (e.g., ToN\_IoT or BoT-IoT) was not evaluated.
    \item \textbf{Payload Inaccessibility}: The dataset strips packet payloads and raw byte streams. Low recall on SQL Injection and XSS reflects the challenge of distinguishing web attacks using statistical flow counters alone.
    \item \textbf{Neural Training Budget}: The PyTorch MLP baseline was trained under a 5-epoch CPU budget. Extended training or specialized tabular neural architectures (e.g., TabNet) may yield different neural baselines.
    \item \textbf{Host Hardware Profiling}: Latency and throughput were measured on an Intel Core i5 host CPU. Performance on constrained physical microcontrollers (e.g., ARM Cortex-M) remains subject to physical hardware constraints.
    \item \textbf{Literature Review Scope}: Methodological comparisons are bounded strictly to the 14 surveyed peer-reviewed studies.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper conducted a data integrity audit and empirical evaluation of machine learning models on the CICIoT2023 benchmark. We demonstrated that standard random splitting---widely adopted across the surveyed benchmark literature---causes extensive coordinate leakage (81.39\% in the unique stratum, 93.00\% in the full sample), creating opportunities for coordinate memorization, while unweighted training optimizes overall accuracy at the expense of minority attack detection, establishing a steep accuracy-versus-minority-recall trade-off. By introducing an atomic SHA-256 group-hash partitioning protocol, we established leak-free diagnostic baselines (89.61\% binary, 78.93\% category, and 72.65\% fine-grained accuracy). We analyzed the operational trade-offs of class weighting, showing that unweighted training trades a 79\% to 82\% reduction in minority attack recall for a +1.80\% gain in headline accuracy. Furthermore, temporal evaluation across distinct capture sessions revealed measurable performance degradation, highlighting the need to evaluate cross-session stability. Future work will investigate physical edge deployment on microcontroller hardware, multi-modal tabular-payload architectures, and cross-dataset evaluation across heterogeneous IoT environments.
